This past spring, I was selected to serve as one of two faculty on the Global Scholars trip to Morocco. While I was not especially familiar with the country's history and culture, I saw it as an opportunity to expand my teaching in Atlantic history.

My colleague Aaron Shreve and I led six pre-trip orientation sessions on Moroccan history and culture. Preparing for these sessions was challenging, as I had never visited Morocco and had spent relatively little time studying it. To assist me, I relied heavily on longtime Harvard historian Susan Gilson Miller's [_A History of Modern Morocco_](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/history-of-modern-morocco/88606965E80CAC57471F2636CAFA885F) (Cambridge, 2013). I also consulted the [_Encyclopedia of Africa_](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/encyclopedia-of-africa-9780195337709) (Oxford, 2010) and documentaries such as [_Africa's Great Civilizations_](https://www.pbs.org/show/africas-great-civilizations/) (PBS, 2017) and [_Lost Kingdoms of Africa_](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HmVPd4f6Qo8) (BBC, 2012).

One of my favorite orientation sessions centered on the screening of [_Casablanca Beats_](https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/casablanca_beats) (2021). The Moroccan submission for the Academy Award for Best International Feature Film in 2021, the film hits a lot of very familiar "beats"; it felt like a Moroccan version of _Footloose_ and _Dead Poets Society_. Yet, the change of setting made it feel fresh. It was a great way for students to think about transnationalism and how the experience of Moroccan college students has key similarities and differences from their own experience at Randolph. It also helped students begin to think more about religion, region, and gender in Morocco. Made by Moroccan filmmakers, the film provided an excellent counterpoint to Hollywood's orientalist narratives.

Throughout our travels, we kept thinking and talking about the film and its themes and how aspects were resonating with our experience.
